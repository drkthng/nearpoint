About the name. The link points to Oryx Neuro’s purchase page for Tim Ferriss listeners. That page blocks automated access, so I could not read it directly. Everywhere else (the company site, its App Store listing, the podcast) the product is called Oryx, tagline “See close. Go far.” I found no separate product named “Proximity”; if that is the name of the Ferriss offer or a program tier, everything below still applies, because the underlying method is the same.

Summary

What it is. Oryx is a tablet and phone app from Sharp Vision Inc. (New York) that trains the visual cortex to cope better with the blur of presbyopia, the age-related loss of focusing range. It is delivered through eye-care professionals and, for the Ferriss audience, as a pilot of 100 places at $999 (usual price $1,300). The program is 30 to 40 daily sessions of up to about 10 minutes, done without reading glasses. Ferriss used it 8 minutes a day for 30 days, went from struggling to read at arm’s length to reading small menu print in dim light, and invested in the company afterwards.

Where it comes from. Oryx is the third commercial generation of a method developed by vision scientist Uri Polat (Tel Aviv, later Bar-Ilan University): first Ucansi, then GlassesOff (2013, roughly one million users), now Oryx. Oryx’s own evidence pages cite Polat’s papers. The core task has not changed in 20 years: detect a faint, briefly flashed Gabor patch that sits between high-contrast “flanker” patches, sometimes inside a crowd of patches and sometimes chased by a mask a few frames later. Difficulty adapts continuously.

How it works. Nothing in the eye changes. In the key study, accommodation, pupil size and depth of focus were identical before and after training. What improves is neural: higher sensitivity to low contrast (the blurred image is a low-contrast image), faster early visual processing, and less crowding between neighbouring letters. Reported gains are near acuity improving by a factor of about 1.5 to 1.6 in minimum angle of resolution (≈0.18 logMAR, about two chart lines) and reading speed at the smallest readable print rising by roughly 17 to 25 words per minute.

How much to believe. The direction of the effect is consistent and related effects appear in independent labs. But nearly all presbyopia data come from the inventor’s group or company employees, control groups were small and not randomised, and I found no sham-controlled randomised trial in presbyopia. Oryx’s headline figures (94 % improved, 2.5–3 lines, durable for 15 years) rest partly on unpublished company data from 19 people. Treat it as a plausible, moderate effect that depends on adherence and has an optical ceiling, not as a replacement for reading glasses in everyone.

Can you build it? Yes. The training protocol is published in enough detail to reimplement. The algorithm is the easy part. The hard parts are display physics (contrast steps finer than 1 %, frame-exact timing, a known viewing distance) and honest validation of whether it works on you. Patents and medical-device law only start to matter if you sell it or make health claims.

Physics: why blur is not the end of the story

Presbyopia is an optical low-pass filter

The crystalline lens stiffens with age, so the eye can no longer add enough optical power for near targets. First complaints about reading typically appear between about 42 and 44, and by around 51 virtually everyone is affected. At a reading distance of 40 cm the eye needs 2.5 D of accommodation; whatever it cannot supply remains as defocus.

In geometric optics, defocus spreads each point into a blur disc whose angular diameter is simply

β [rad] ≈ p [m] × ΔD [D]
p  = pupil diameter
ΔD = uncorrected defocus in dioptres

That gives the following blur-disc sizes in arcminutes:

Pupil0.5 D1.0 D1.5 D2.0 D
2 mm (bright light)3.4′6.9′10.3′13.8′
3 mm (typical indoor)5.2′10.3′15.5′20.6′
4 mm (dim restaurant)6.9′13.8′20.6′27.5′

For scale: newspaper text at 40 cm has letters of about 14′ and strokes of about 2.8′. Two consequences follow directly. First, dim light hurts twice: the pupil dilates, which enlarges the blur disc linearly, and lower luminance lowers contrast sensitivity. That is exactly the dim-menu scenario Ferriss described. Second, the residual defocus is smaller than the reading add itself, because an add is prescribed on top of whatever accommodation remains; the table is a per-dioptre ruler, not a mapping from prescription to blur.

What blur does in frequency space

A uniform blur disc of angular diameter β (in degrees) has the modulation transfer function

MTF(f) = 2·J₁(π β f) / (π β f)
first zero at f₀ ≈ 1.22 / β     (β in degrees, f in cycles/degree)

The important point for a physicist: below the first zero, blur attenuates contrast rather than deleting information. With a 3 mm pupil and 1 D of residual defocus, a 4 cycles/degree component keeps about 52 % of its contrast and a 6 cycles/degree component about 15 %, while the first zero sits near 7 cycles/degree. If the observer’s neural contrast threshold drops, components that were sub-threshold become usable again. That is the physical opening the training exploits, and it is also its ceiling: beyond the first zero (and with phase reversals) no amount of neural gain recovers the signal.

Two empirical findings fit this picture. Watson and Ahumada showed that blur detection and discrimination behave like contrast detection, so improving contrast detection should improve the handling of blur. And in the 119-person study the improvement was, on average, about 0.18 logMAR regardless of where people ended up, roughly a constant factor of 1.5 relative to post-training acuity. The authors read this as a fundamental optical limit: the brain learns to interpret the blurred image more efficiently, but it cannot exceed what the optics deliver.

The stimulus: a Gabor patch

Every training trial is built from Gabor patches, a sinusoidal grating under a Gaussian window. In luminance terms:

L(x, y) = L₀ · [ 1 + C · exp(−(x² + y²) / 2σ²) · cos(2π f·u + φ) ]
u = x·cos θ + y·sin θ        C = Michelson contrast, f = spatial frequency, θ = carrier direction

Gabors are used because they are compact in both space and spatial frequency, which matches the receptive fields of orientation- and frequency-tuned neurons in primary visual cortex (V1); a Gabor drives a narrow population of those neurons efficiently. In the published protocol, σ is set so that at least two cycles are visible, spatial frequencies span 2–8 cycles/degree, patch sizes run from 0.18° to 1°, and four orientations are used (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°).

Display physics you cannot skip

Geometry

At 40 cm one degree of visual angle is 6.98 mm on the screen. The 2016 iOS study used a pixel pitch of 0.078 mm, so about 89 pixels per degree, or about 11 pixels per cycle at 8 cycles/degree. Current phones and tablets have finer pitch, so sampling is not the problem. Distance is: every centimetre of error changes the delivered spatial frequency proportionally, which is why the study fixed 40 cm and why you need a way to measure it.

Contrast resolution

Thresholds for trained observers fall to a few percent and below. An 8-bit display cannot represent that well: the GlassesOff authors note that with 256 grey levels the smallest luminance step near mid-grey exceeds 1 %, and they capped the lowest contrast just above 1 %. Psychophysics labs have long worked around this. Tyler’s bit-stealing mixes slightly different R, G and B levels to obtain about 1,786 luminance steps from an 8-bit colour display. The noisy-bit method adds a small random offset before quantisation, which makes the average luminance effectively continuous; it was shown not to affect contrast thresholds at ordinary resolutions. A GPU shader makes either trivial.

Timing

Durations must be counted in frames. All values in the 2016 study are frame multiples at 60 Hz: target durations of 34, 68 and 116 ms are 2, 4 and 7 frames; mask delays of 68, 83, 116 and 150 ms are 4, 5, 7 and 9 frames. On 120 Hz displays, refresh can change dynamically, so you must lock or measure it. Browser-based timing is usable but not lab-grade: in a large timing comparison, the best web tools reached a few milliseconds of precision, while native lab software stayed below one millisecond.

Luminance and environment

The study set display luminance to maximum (about 120 cd/m² on the devices used) in an otherwise dark room. On a modern phone you must also disable auto-brightness, colour-temperature features (True Tone, Night Shift) and anything that remaps tones. OLED panels can flicker at low brightness, another reason to train at full brightness.

Biology: what exactly is being trained

The V1 front end

Neurons in primary visual cortex respond to local oriented contrast at a preferred spatial frequency. Blur from presbyopia feeds them a weaker, lower-contrast signal; because contrast drives cortical responses, the result is weaker and slower responses, and letters become harder and slower to identify. The training does not try to sharpen the retinal image. It tries to make this cortical machinery extract more from the degraded input.

Lateral interactions: the collinear trick

A faint target Gabor becomes easier to detect when two high-contrast Gabors of the same orientation are placed along its axis, roughly two to four wavelengths (λ) away. Closer than about 2λ, especially with non-collinear flankers or a high-contrast target, the effect turns into suppression. Recordings in cat and monkey V1 show collinear flankers increasing responses to a central target, most of all when the target is near threshold. The leading explanation is the network of long-range horizontal connections that links V1 neurons with similar orientation preference, with feedback from higher areas probably contributing at larger distances. One psychophysical estimate of the propagation speed, about 0.1 m/s, matches the slow conduction of those horizontal fibres.

Why train on this? Polat’s argument is that practising detection at the edge of facilitation strengthens these interactions: a patent describing the method reports an increased range of excitatory interactions and reduced inhibition after training, in humans and monkeys. A stronger, cleaner lateral network raises effective gain for low-contrast input, which is what a blurred letter is.

Time: backward masking and processing speed

In the masking conditions, the flankers (or a crowd of patches) reappear shortly after the target. A mask at around 50 ms after the target cancels collinear facilitation, so the observer succeeds only if the target has been processed before the mask interferes. Training pushes that window shorter. In young adults, a similar protocol shortened the latency of an ERP marker by about 20 ms. In the 2014 study, the time needed to reach 6/6 acuity under crowded conditions fell by 81 ms, with only subtle change in contrast sensitivity. Oryx’s marketing line about capturing “more visual samples per second” is a lay translation of this result.

Crowding

Crowding, the failure to recognise an object among clutter, is classically a peripheral phenomenon, but Polat’s group reports it in the fovea of presbyopes and finds it scales with acuity in ageing eyes. Reading is the ultimate crowded task: letters within words, words within lines. The training therefore includes arrays of 4–11 flankers, and its gains transfer to crowded letter charts and to reading speed.

Perceptual learning in general

Perceptual learning, improvement through practice on a demanding perceptual task, is classically specific to the trained orientation, location and stimulus, which was taken as evidence for plasticity in early visual cortex. Later work showed that specificity depends on the procedure: task precision, difficulty, number of trials and schedule all matter, and “double training” (practising a second, seemingly irrelevant task at another location) can produce complete transfer. Models describe learning as retuning of early representations, reweighting of their read-out, or both. For a builder this has a practical meaning: vary orientation, spatial frequency and spacing across sessions, keep difficulty near threshold, and do not drill one stimulus to exhaustion.

What is not happening

  • No optical change: accommodation, pupil size and depth of focus were measured objectively before and after training and did not change.
  • Not “eye exercises”. A study of a popular read-without-glasses exercise program found no objective improvement in near vision, even though most participants said their vision had improved. An independent Korean study found that alternating fixation between near and far screens worked no better, and if anything slightly worse, than training at a fixed distance.
  • Not blur adaptation. The authors argue blur adaptation is short-lived and would already have solved presbyopic reading if it were enough; they favour increased neural gain (“de-blurring”) plus faster processing.

This is a working miniature of the task, drawn pixel by pixel in linear luminance, encoded to sRGB and dithered with the noisy-bit method. It measures your display’s refresh rate and counts durations in frames. Verdict

  • The direction of the effect is consistent across devices, ages and several labs, including independent groups using different tasks.
  • The mechanism story is coherent: better contrast detection, faster processing and less crowding, with the eye’s optics demonstrably unchanged.
  • The dose is modest: roughly 25–40 sessions; in the 119-person data neither device type nor 12- versus 30-minute sessions changed the gain.
  • I found no sham-controlled randomised trial in presbyopia. Controls were 3 and 10 people, not randomised, so test familiarity and expectation are not fully excluded.
  • Most presbyopia data come from people paid by, employed by or holding stock in the companies. Ferriss disclosed that he is an investor.
  • Durability: the 15-year maintenance claim rests on 19 people and unpublished company data.
  • Outcomes were measured on charts under standard lighting; benefit in dim, real-world reading is plausible but less documented.
  • The gain has an optical ceiling. With adds above about +1.50 D, complete independence from readers is unlikely.

A precedent worth knowing: in 2015–16 the FTC required the makers of UltimEyes, another vision-training app, to stop claiming it was scientifically proven to improve vision, fined them $150,000, and objected that the cited research was run by a co-owner without disclosure. Researchers in the field protested that the FTC’s bar (including double-blind studies) was higher than the field’s norms. Either way, it shows where the regulatory line sits for health claims.

Physicist’s reading: this is a gain-and-speed adjustment of a noisy detector looking through a low-pass filter. It can buy about one and a half to two chart lines when the filter still passes the relevant frequencies at reduced contrast. It cannot restore frequencies past the first MTF zero. That makes it most useful for early presbyopes, for dim-light reading and for delaying the first pair of readers, which is also what Oryx’s own suitability rules say.

Core code

Gabor fragment shader with noisy-bit dithering (WebGL 1 / GLSL ES)

precision highp float;
uniform vec2  uCenter;   // patch centre in device pixels
uniform float uLambda;   // pixels per cycle = pxPerDeg / cpd
uniform float uSigma;    // pixels (e.g. = uLambda for ~2 visible cycles)
uniform float uTheta;    // carrier direction in radians
uniform float uC;        // Michelson contrast of this patch
uniform float uMean;     // mean luminance, linear 0..1
uniform float uGamma;    // fitted display gamma (replace with measured LUT if you can)
uniform float uSeed;     // new value every frame

float hash(vec2 p) {
  return fract(sin(dot(p, vec2(12.9898, 78.233)) + uSeed) * 43758.5453);
}

void main() {
  vec2  d = gl_FragCoord.xy - uCenter;
  float u = d.x * cos(uTheta) + d.y * sin(uTheta);
  float g = exp(-dot(d, d) / (2.0 * uSigma * uSigma)) * cos(6.2831853 * u / uLambda);
  float L = uMean * (1.0 + uC * g);                    // linear luminance
  float code = 255.0 * pow(clamp(L, 0.0, 1.0), 1.0 / uGamma);
  code = floor(code + hash(gl_FragCoord.xy));          // stochastic rounding: E[code] exact
  gl_FragColor = vec4(vec3(code / 255.0), 1.0);
}
// For flankers and crowding: sum g over an array of patch uniforms before computing L.

Staircase and a simulated-observer test (Python, verified for this report)

import math, random

class Staircase:
    """2-down / 1-up on log10 contrast; converges near 70.7 % correct."""
    def __init__(self, c0=0.2, step=0.1, floor=0.01, ceil=0.9):
        self.logc, self.step = math.log10(c0), step
        self.lo, self.hi = math.log10(floor), math.log10(ceil)
        self.streak, self.last_dir, self.reversals = 0, 0, []

    @property
    def contrast(self):
        return 10 ** self.logc

    def update(self, correct: bool):
        if correct:
            self.streak += 1
            if self.streak < 2:
                return
            self.streak, direction = 0, -1
        else:
            self.streak, direction = 0, +1
        if self.last_dir and direction != self.last_dir:
            self.reversals.append(self.logc)
        self.last_dir = direction
        self.logc = min(self.hi, max(self.lo, self.logc + direction * self.step))

    def threshold(self, n=8):
        r = self.reversals[-n:]
        return 10 ** (sum(r) / len(r)) if r else None


def weibull(c, alpha=0.04, beta=3.0, guess=0.5, lapse=0.02):
    return guess + (1 - guess - lapse) * (1 - math.exp(-(c / alpha) ** beta))


def test_staircase_recovers_simulated_threshold():
    # true 70.7 % point of this observer is ~0.033
    ests = []
    for seed in range(200):
        rng = random.Random(seed)
        s = Staircase(c0=0.3)
        for _ in range(300):
            s.update(rng.random() < weibull(s.contrast))
        ests.append(s.threshold())
    med = sorted(ests)[len(ests) // 2]
    assert 0.028 < med < 0.040, med      # observed median: 0.031

Run here: median estimate 0.031 against a true value of 0.033, with a 5–95 % range of 0.019–0.041 over 200 simulated runs of 300 trials. That spread is the honest noise floor of a single block, and it is why you should judge progress on session trends, not single numbers.

Working with Claude Code

Give Claude Code the protocol as data and the physics as non-negotiable rules, then build module by module behind tests. A starter CLAUDE.md:

# Near-vision perceptual-learning trainer (personal research use)

## Non-negotiables
- Every stimulus duration is an integer number of frames at the measured refresh rate.
- Log intended vs. presented frame timestamps for every trial; trials with drops are void.
- Contrast is specified in linear luminance, encoded through the display LUT, then dithered.
- All geometry is in degrees of visual angle, converted with measured px/mm and distance.
- Training stimuli and outcome tests never share code paths or stimuli.
- Every change to adaptive logic ships with a simulated-observer test.

## Reference
- docs/protocol.md   parameter table (Polat 2012, Yehezkel 2016)
- docs/curriculum.json
- docs/calibration.md

Prompts that work well in sequence:

  1. “Read docs/protocol.md. Plan a PsychoPy implementation of SINGLE, LM, CROWD and BM-LM with frame-based timing. List open questions before writing code.”
  2. “Implement the stimulus renderer with linear-light summation and noisy-bit dithering. Tests: mean code-value error over 100,000 pixels below 0.05; pixels-per-degree within 0.5 % of the reference calculation.”
  3. “Implement the staircase and a Weibull simulated observer. Report bias and spread of the threshold estimate for 50-, 100- and 210-trial blocks over 500 runs.”
  4. “Implement the curriculum engine from docs/curriculum.json with property-based tests for the rule invariants (one parameter per session, orientation rotation, minimum sessions).”
  5. “Port the renderer to Metal with CADisplayLink and ARKit face-distance tracking; write an XCTest that fails if more than 1 % of trials drop a frame.”

Validate the instrument

  • Timing: film the screen with a phone’s 240 fps slow-motion mode (about 4 ms per frame) and count presented frames of a test flash against the log.
  • Luminance: measure about 17 grey levels with a photometer or colorimeter, fit gamma, and verify the dithered mean of a uniform patch.
  • Geometry: render a 10° bar and measure it at 40 cm with a ruler.
  • Adaptive logic: simulated observers with known thresholds, slopes and lapse rates, run in CI.

Run it on yourself properly

  1. Get an eye exam first to rule out cataract, glaucoma or macular disease, and to know your add.
  2. Measure a baseline twice, a week apart: near letter acuity at 40 cm on a printed chart under fixed lighting (include a dim condition), reading speed at the smallest print you can read, and contrast thresholds. Use two chart versions so you cannot memorise letters.
  3. Decide in advance what counts as success. The 2016 paper quotes MNREAD repeatability of about ±0.05 logMAR and ±8.6 words per minute; a real effect should clearly exceed that.
  4. Train 30–40 sessions at a fixed time and place, without readers.
  5. Post-test with the identical procedure, ideally with someone else running the chart, then retest after 4–8 weeks without training to check retention.

Pitfalls

  • Training with readers on, or at a drifting distance: both change the stimulus the brain actually sees.
  • Variable refresh (120 Hz panels dropping to 60 Hz in low-power mode) silently doubling your durations.
  • Auto-brightness, True Tone, Night Shift or dark-mode tone mapping changing luminance between sessions.
  • Using the training task as the outcome measure; it will always look like progress.
  • Overtraining in one sitting. Published sessions were 12–30 minutes; Oryx caps at about 10.

IP and regulation

None of this restricts building and using a trainer for yourself. It matters if you share, sell or make health claims. This is orientation, not legal advice.

  • Patents. The original Polat and Ucansi family dates from about 1999–2005 (for example WO 2001/047463 on improving visual perception and US 8,403,485 on vision evaluation; related patents include US 6,876,758 and US 7,427,138). With 20-year terms, the early ones have probably lapsed, but GlassesOff or Oryx may hold newer filings, and the University of California holds US 10,149,795 (granted 2018) on Andersen’s contrast-training method. Commercial use needs a freedom-to-operate search.
  • Medical device law. In the EU, software whose intended purpose is to treat or alleviate a condition such as presbyopia is likely a medical device under the MDR and needs CE marking; in Germany the DiGA route exists for reimbursed digital therapeutics. Calling it “wellness” does not help if the claims are medical.
  • Advertising claims. The UltimEyes case shows US enforcement; in Germany the Heilmittelwerbegesetz restricts health advertising. Claims need competent, disclosed evidence.
